Data Centers, Water, and Power: The Numbers You Should See

Big computer warehouses are coming to our region. Here is how much water and electricity they would use, how much we have, and the one question to ask before any deal is approved.

Independent community explainer prepared by Celaya Solutions Research LLC. This is not an official City of El Paso publication. Every number below comes from a public source, listed at the end. Check them yourself.

A data center is a giant building full of computers. The computers run hot, so they need water and power to stay cool and switched on. The question is simple: do we have enough for this, and who pays if we do not?

The data centers would use only about 1.5% of the water we use now. That sounds tiny. But it is 17.9% of the cushion we have left. Picture a paycheck: after rent and bills you have $87 left, and someone asks for $16. That is 1.6% of your pay, but 18% of what is left. That is the difference between making it to the end of the month and not.

Two of the new data centers alone (Meta in El Paso and Wiwynn in Socorro) would add about 250 megawatts of demand. That is roughly double the cushion we have. To handle it, the utility has proposed $3.5 billion in upgrades and a $22-a-month rate increase. The question is who should pay for that.

The big question nobody answered

"Which water-supply number are you using when you say we have enough water for data centers, and where is it published?"

If we count the water we actually pump and treat each day, we are already near the limit. If we count a bigger paper number, there is room. No official has said which number they used. If they cannot answer with a source you can check, the decision was based on trust, not math. Trust is not a water source.

What a fair rule would do

A draft ordinance (a city law) would set clear terms for any large data center, the same for every company:

  • Require a public hearing and Council vote (a special permit), not a quiet approval.
  • Keep them 1,000 feet from homes, schools, parks, hospitals, and churches.
  • Cap their water, require recycled water for cooling, and measure and publish use every quarter.
  • Make them pay 100% of the power and water upgrades they cause, so your bill does not rise to cover them.
  • Require a cleanup bond before they open, so an empty building never becomes the public's problem.
  • Fine them daily and pull the permit if they break the rules.
